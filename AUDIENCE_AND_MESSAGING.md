# Audience profile and messaging guide

Status: working document. Much of this is judgment, not data. Facts from research are marked (source: ...) and come from third-party summaries; verify before relying on them. Update this after real conversations: your Problems Log beats any assumption here.

## 1. Who we're talking to

### Primary audience: parents (they buy)
- Parents and guardians considering or already doing homeschooling, flexible online school, or acceleration.
- Age mix and platform habits: NOT verified. Don't assume. Ask early subscribers (optional one-question survey) and track where signups come from.
- What research suggests (indicative only):
  - A homeschool publisher's survey reported over 60% of surveyed homeschool parents use homeschool blogs when choosing curriculum, and 97% read product reviews (source: The Old Schoolhouse industry survey; vendor-oriented).
  - A survey of 1,002 parents found just over half trust parenting information on social media versus over 80% for authoritative websites, and 65% said parents' stories would increase trust (source: Zero to Three; about parenting advice, mostly parents of young children).
  - Marketing blogs claim many millennial parents check reviews on their phones and prefer brand email; these are directional only.
- What they probably want (to test): reassurance they aren't harming their child, a clear way to decide, a plan, and proof it can work.
- What they probably fear (to test): damaging their child socially or academically, "school at home" that isn't working, pushing too hard, legal or paperwork mistakes, being judged.

### Secondary audience: students and teens (they influence, they don't buy)
- Teens using the guide with a parent, or who see your content.
- Data (source: Pew, fall 2025, 1,458 US teens): 92% use YouTube; TikTok and Instagram roughly 63% and 61% per a secondary source; about 1 in 5 say they're on TikTok or YouTube almost constantly. Nothing reliable found on how they respond to ads or influencers.
- Our rule: don't sell to minors and don't collect their information. Give them content that helps them talk to their parents.

## 3. Messaging for parents

**Core promise:** "Homeschooling can be built around your child. Here's what worked for me, what was hard, and how to check if it's working."

**Tone:** warm, calm, honest, specific. Like a thoughtful older cousin who's been through it. Not a guru.

**Do**
- Lead with your real story: left school in 3rd grade, high school at 13, associate at 15, bachelor's at 17, master's in progress.
- Be honest about the hard parts (first year, missing friends, the freedom).
- Give one useful tool per message (a question, a checklist, a check-in).
- Show proof (blurred diploma and transcripts) and say what you don't know.
- Use "my experience," "for me," "every child is different."
- Invite, don't push: "happy to share the checklist."
- Use real parent questions and their words (with permission).

**Don't**
- Promise outcomes, speed or income. No "graduate by 13."
- Use hype, scarcity, countdown timers or fear.
- Say "the right way" as a rule for others. Say what you did and why.
- Criticize families who do it differently.
- Share private details about your parents, brothers or other families.
- Give medical, mental-health or legal advice.

**Hooks that fit this audience (tests, not facts)**
- "What about socialization? My honest answer."
- "My first year of online school was rough. Here's what I wish my parents knew."
- "Three signs your homeschool is just school at home."
- "Ready or pushed? How to tell."

**Sample lines**
- Newsletter opener: "Here's something I wish someone had told my parents in my first year."
- Group reply: "I can only speak from my experience, but..."
- Offer: "If it would help to talk it through, I offer a free 30-minute chat. No pitch."

## 4. Messaging for teens and students

**Core promise:** "Your learning can fit you. Here's how I told my parents what wasn't working, and what changed."

**Tone:** direct, real, light humor allowed, never preachy. Short.

**Do**
- Short clips with a clear hook in the first seconds.
- Share the awkward parts (the shock when classmates learned my age).
- End with "send this to a parent" or "try this with your parent."
- Give them a way to say things: "What works for me is __. What doesn't is __."

**Don't**
- Push them to buy or collect their email or details.
- Pressure them to go faster, or glamorize early graduation.
- Imply a teen should overrule their parents.

**Hooks (tests)**
- "How I told my parents I missed school."
- "Nobody tells you about the first year of online school."
- "Finished high school at 13, but that's not the point."

## 5. Moving from content to selling (parents)

1. **Free:** checklist, answers, newsletter, free chats.
2. **Low cost:** guide and planner (price to test).
3. **Higher touch:** paid roadmap session, later a small cohort.
4. **Sell only after trust:** a parent should have seen several useful things from you first.
5. **Offer, don't pressure:** one clear next step per message.
6. **Ask for reviews and testimonials** after a good experience, with written permission. Parents tend to read reviews.
7. **Be clear about refunds and limits** (not a teacher, counselor or lawyer; no guaranteed results).

## 6. What to measure and test (weekly)
- New subscribers and which source each came from.
- Replies and questions (log in the Problems Log).
- Free chats booked and completed.
- Which posts or groups bring signups.
- Optional one-question survey to subscribers: "Where do you usually look for homeschool advice?" (use the answers to replace guesses here).
