## The question under the question

When families hear that I graduated high school at 13, the first question is usually some version of: **"How do I know if my child is ready?"**

It's a good question. But I'd like to change it slightly, because "ready" can quietly turn into "fast." What I actually want you to ask is:

> **"Is this the right fit for my child right now, and how will we know if it stops being the right fit?"**

Speed is a result. Fit is the goal. In my case, moving fast happened *because* the experience was built around me. It wasn't the starting point.

## Two kinds of readiness

When people talk about readiness, they usually mean academics. But there are at least two kinds, and the second matters just as much.

### 1. Academic readiness
Can your child do the work comfortably, and do they understand it, not just finish it?

Signs it might be there:
- They finish material easily and ask for more.
- They can explain ideas in their own words.
- They're curious and chase questions beyond the assignment.

A caution: finishing quickly is not the same as understanding deeply. Moving fast can leave gaps in the foundations, especially in subjects that build on each other like math and writing. We'll look at how to check for gaps in Chapter 5.

### 2. Emotional and social readiness
Can your child handle the *experience* of what's ahead, being among older students, handling harder expectations, managing their own time?

Signs it might be there:
- They can tell you when something is hard instead of hiding it.
- They can bounce back from a bad day without it spiralling.
- They have people they can talk to, friends, mentors, family.
- They actually *want* it, not just agree because you do.

For me, the second kind was shaped a lot by how my family worked. I gave feedback on what worked and what didn't, and my parents listened. [ADD: one moment where you told your parents something wasn't working and they changed it. Even a small one.]

## Readiness vs. pressure

This is the part I most want you to read carefully.

From the outside, a child who is ready and a child who is being pushed can look similar: both are working hard, both are doing well. The difference is in how it *feels* and what happens when things get tough.

| | Readiness | Pressure |
|---|---|---|
| Where it comes from | The child's curiosity and pace | An outside goal (a date, a comparison, a headline) |
| Who's asking for it | The child, often | Mostly the adults |
| When something is hard | The child can say so and the plan changes | The child hides it or the plan doesn't change |
| After a setback | They bounce back | They shut down or dread the work |
| Rest and downtime | Protected | Squeezed out |
| What success means | Learning, understanding, progress | Finishing, being "ahead" |

If you see yourself on the right side, that's information, not a failing. Most of us start there because we care. The good news is you can move to the left side by changing how you run things.

## Warning signs to watch for

None of these prove anything alone, and I'm not a doctor or counselor. But if you see several, slow down and talk.

- Dread or tears around schoolwork that wasn't there before.
- Losing interest in things they used to love.
- Constant tiredness, headaches or stomach aches with no clear cause (talk to a doctor if so).
- Cutting out friends and activities to "keep up."
- Perfectionism, being afraid to make mistakes.
- Praise being tied mainly to speed, so they feel their worth is how fast they finish.

If any of these seem serious, or you're worried about your child's well-being, please talk to a qualified professional. That's beyond what this book can do.

## What helped me

I can only speak to my own case, but here's what I think mattered:

1. **I had a say.** I wasn't a passenger in my own education. I told my parents what was and wasn't working.
2. **I could work at my own pace**, not a faster pace than I wanted, my pace.
3. **I had an online instructor** alongside that pace, so I wasn't alone with the material.
4. **I had a social life.** I was in local high school clubs and organizations and church groups, so my days weren't only school.
5. **It was hard at first, and that was allowed.** I hated my first year of online school. Nobody pretended I shouldn't.

## A simple way to check in, every month

Sit down together, relaxed, and ask:

1. What was the best part of the last month?
2. What was the hardest?
3. Is there anything you'd like to change about how we do this?
4. On a scale of 1-5, how are you feeling about school right now?
5. Do you want to go faster, slower, or stay the same, and why?

Write down the answers. Change one thing. Check again next month.

If the answers start trending in the wrong direction, that's the signal to slow down, no matter how well the grades look.

## For students

- Being able to do the work doesn't mean you have to do all of it at the maximum speed.
- It's okay to say "I want to slow down" and it's okay to say "I want to go faster." Both are valid.
- If you're tired in a way that doesn't go away, tell a parent or another adult you trust.
- Your feelings are part of the plan, not an obstacle to it.
