I graduated high school at 13. I'm also going to tell you that for many children, **moving fast is not the right answer**, and that's okay.

Acceleration is a tool. Like any tool, it's great for some jobs and wrong for others. This chapter is about knowing the difference.

## When slowing down or staying put is better

**1. The child doesn't want it.** If the motivation is mostly yours, a date, a comparison, pride, the plan will crack the first time it gets hard. A child who has to be convinced every week is telling you something.

**2. Depth is what they need.** Some kids are not looking to finish sooner. They want to go *deeper*: spend three months on one topic, build something, read ten books on one period of history. That is a fine, even excellent, use of homeschooling. Early graduation was my outcome. It's not the only good one.

**3. There are gaps.** If they're moving fast but the foundations are shaky (see Chapter 5), more speed adds weight to a weak base.

**4. The rest of life would be squeezed out.** If friends, activities, sleep and downtime would be the price, that's too high.

**5. They're burned out or close to it.** Dread, tears, shutting down. Pause first, plan later. (If you're worried about their well-being, talk to a qualified professional.)

**6. The goal is a destination you haven't checked.** Before racing to a milestone like college, ask: what's waiting there, and is it what we want?

## What to do instead

You don't have to choose between "fast" and "failing." There's a lot of room in between:

- **Go deeper, not faster.** Projects, long reading, hands-on work.
- **Accelerate one subject, not all of them.** A child can be ahead in math and right on pace in writing.
- **Slow down on purpose.** Give a hard semester more time. That's a plan, not a failure.
- **Add enrichment.** Courses in things that aren't "required": art, coding, music, languages, entrepreneurship.
- **Build the social side.** Clubs, community groups, church or volunteer groups.
- **Take a planned break.** Rest is part of learning.

My own path included moving quickly, but it also included space to follow ideas I was curious about. With school ending around 2 each day, I had the rest of the afternoon for other school activities and things I was interested in, whether that was a trip to the park or something else. ## Pausing doesn't mean you failed

If you slow down, you haven't wasted anything. You've listened to what your child needs. That's the skill this whole book is about.

## Tool: Pause or continue?

Answer yes or no together.

| Question | Yes | No |
|---|---|---|
| Does the learner want to continue at this speed? | | |
| Do they understand the material, not only finish it? | | |
| Are they sleeping, eating and socializing normally? | | |
| Do they have at least one adult to talk to about stress? | | |
| Is there room in the week for something that's just fun? | | |

- **Mostly yes:** continue and check in monthly.
- **Mixed:** change one thing, then recheck in two weeks.
- **Mostly no:** slow down, then talk about what's driving the pace.

## For students

Wanting to slow down is allowed. Wanting to go deep instead of fast is allowed. You're not behind. You're building something.
